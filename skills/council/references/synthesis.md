# Dialectical Synthesis Methodology

This is not summarization. This is synthesis: finding the signal in the noise, the tensions beneath the agreement, and the gaps nobody noticed.

## Adapting to Council Size

- **2 members:** Skip consensus thresholds. Report agreement or disagreement directly. Focus on the single most important tension and on blind spots. No Confidence Map.
- **3–4 members:** Full synthesis. Consensus means all, or all but one, agree. A tension is any two members meaningfully disagreeing.
- **5–6 members:** Full synthesis with proportional thresholds. Consensus means more than 60% agree. Prioritize the 2–3 most significant tensions rather than cataloging every disagreement.

## Reading the Cross-Examination

When round 2 ran, it is your best evidence. Before the seven steps, sort what happened in it:

- **Held under challenge:** positions someone attacked directly that survived with a real answer. These are the council's strongest findings.
- **Unchallenged:** points everyone asserted and nobody tested. This is agreement, but untested. Don't present it as being as strong as the above.
- **Moved:** members who SHARPENED or CHANGED, and what moved them. When a member changes their mind, it often points straight at the crux.
- **Dissolved:** round-1 tensions that turned out to be misunderstandings once members read each other. Mention them briefly or drop them.
- **Persisted:** disagreements that survived direct engagement. These are the genuine trade-offs, the real decision points.
- **Settling tests:** the facts, tests, or scenarios members named as what would settle a challenge. The cheap ones make excellent next steps.

Treat each member's round-2 position and confidence as their final word. Round 1 is the reasoning behind it.

## The 7-Step Synthesis Process

### Step 1: Map Consensus
Identify findings where a **strong majority of members agree**, using their final positions.

**Proportional thresholds:**
- 2 members: both agree
- 3 members: all 3 agree
- 4 members: 3+ agree
- 5 members: 3+ agree
- 6 members: 4+ agree (>60%)

State consensus points clearly and concisely. When cross-examination ran, mark each one as *held under challenge* or *unchallenged*. Without cross-examination, still say whether the agreement is substantive ("we each analyzed this and reached the same conclusion") or superficial ("nobody questioned this assumption").

### Step 2: Identify Tensions
Find where **2 or more members meaningfully disagree**. For each tension:
- Name the underlying **values in conflict** (e.g. "speed vs. correctness", "simplicity vs. flexibility", "user experience vs. technical elegance").
- State each side's argument with intellectual honesty, as strongly as its advocates would.
- When cross-examination ran, prefer tensions that **persisted** through it, and cite the exchange: who challenged whom, and how they answered.
- Don't resolve it immediately. First make sure the tension is clearly understood.

**At 5–6 members:** many pairwise disagreements are possible. Focus on tensions that are **structurally important**, meaning disagreements about core values or approach rather than surface differences. Limit yourself to the 2–3 most significant.

### Step 3: Resolve or Frame Tensions
For each tension from Step 2, decide which kind it is:
- **Resolvable:** Is it a false dichotomy? Could a different framing satisfy both values? If so, propose that resolution.
- **Genuine trade-off:** Must one value win? Then state the trade-off clearly: "If you prioritize X, you accept Y. If you prioritize Z, you accept W." Let the user decide based on their priorities.
- **Context-dependent:** Does the right answer depend on information you don't have? Name that information, and say how it would change the recommendation. If a member proposed a settling test, use it.

### Step 4: Detect Blind Spots
This is the most valuable step. Review the original question and ask:
- What aspects of the problem did **no member address**?
- What **assumptions went unchallenged** by everyone, in both rounds?
- What **stakeholders or affected parties** were not considered?
- What **failure modes** were not explored?
- What **time horizons** were not examined?

Name these blind spots explicitly. A blind spot doesn't mean the analysis is wrong. It means there's unexplored territory that could change the recommendation.

### Step 5: Build Confidence Map
**Skip this step for 2-member councils.**

For each major aspect of the question, aggregate the members' final confidence ratings:
- **High confidence:** a strong majority rated this area High, with no Low ratings, and ideally it held under challenge.
- **Medium confidence:** mixed ratings, or only a minority of members addressed this area.
- **Low confidence:** any member rated it Low, or a significant disagreement persisted.
- **Unknown:** no member addressed this area (a blind spot).

Note where confidence moved during cross-examination. Present the map as a table for quick scanning.

### Step 6: Synthesize Verdict
Write a 1–3 sentence bottom-line recommendation that:
- accounts for the strongest arguments from each member,
- acknowledges the most important tension and how to navigate it,
- is actionable: the reader should know what to DO, not just what to THINK.

Don't hedge with "it depends" without saying what it depends ON.

### Step 7: Order Next Steps
Propose 3–5 concrete next steps, ordered by:
1. Urgency: what must happen first?
2. Confidence: start with high-confidence actions.
3. Information value: which action would resolve the most uncertainty? A cheap settling test from cross-examination usually belongs early.

Each step should be specific enough that someone could act on it immediately.

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Summarization masquerading as synthesis:** "The Architect said X, the Skeptic said Y, the Pragmatist said Z" is a summary, not a synthesis. Find the connections, tensions, and emergent insights.
- **False balance:** Not every member is equally relevant to every question. Give more weight to the members whose expertise applies most.
- **Counting votes:** Every member is the same underlying model wearing a different lens, so agreement is weaker evidence than a head count suggests. Weigh how independent their *reasons* are. Three routes to the same conclusion through different mechanisms is strong; three phrasings of one argument is one opinion.
- **Presenting confidence as calibration:** Members rate themselves. The Confidence Map aggregates those self-ratings, and it should say so rather than imply measured accuracy.
- **Premature consensus:** Don't smooth over genuine disagreements. Tensions are information.
- **Missing the meta-pattern:** Sometimes the most important insight is the SHAPE of the disagreement, not its content. If the Architect and the Pragmatist always disagree, that itself says something about the problem.
- **Treating round 2 as a formality:** If nobody moved and nobody conceded anything, say so. It means either strong agreement or members talking past each other, and the report should say which.
- **Tension overload (5–6 members):** With more members, resist cataloging every disagreement. Surface the 2–3 tensions that actually matter for the decision at hand.
